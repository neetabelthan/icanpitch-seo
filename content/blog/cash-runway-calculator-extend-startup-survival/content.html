What Is Cash Runway? The Definition That Determines Your Survival

Cash runway is the number of months your startup can operate before running out of money, assuming your current burn rate and no additional revenue growth or fundraising.

Basic Formula: Cash Runway (in months) = Current Cash Balance ÷ Monthly Net Burn Rate

Marcus's initial calculation was stark:

  • Current cash balance: $640,000
  • Monthly net burn rate: $80,000
  • Runway: $640,000 ÷ $80,000 = 8 months

This simple calculation revealed an uncomfortable truth: Marcus had to either raise money, dramatically reduce costs, or accelerate revenue—all within the next 3-4 months to avoid running out of cash.

  • Customer concerns: Enterprise customers hesitate to buy from companies that might disappear

How to Calculate Your Cash Runway: Step-by-Step Guide

Let's walk through the exact process Marcus used, which works whether you're pre-revenue or generating millions in ARR.

Step 1: Determine Your Current Cash Balance

Your cash balance includes:

  • Operating account balance: Day-to-day checking account
  • Savings or money market accounts: Low-risk liquid reserves
  • Short-term investments: Treasury bills, CDs maturing within 90 days

Do NOT include:

  • Accounts receivable (money customers owe but haven't paid)
  • Inventory value
  • Long-term investments or restricted cash
  • Verbal funding commitments (only count cash actually wired to your account)

Marcus's calculation:

  • Operating account: $485,000
  • Money market account: $155,000
  • Total liquid cash: $640,000

According to Silicon Valley Bank's 2024 Startup Outlook Report, the median cash balance for seed-stage startups is $1.2M, while Series A companies hold $4.8M on average.

Step 2: Calculate Your Monthly Net Burn Rate

As covered in our detailed guide on how to calculate burn rate, your net burn rate is:

Net Burn Rate = Monthly Operating Expenses - Monthly Revenue

Marcus's numbers:

  • Monthly operating expenses: $142,000
  • Monthly recurring revenue: $62,000
  • Net monthly burn: $142,000 - $62,000 = $80,000

Important: Use your 3-month average burn rate for accuracy, not just last month's number. Marcus's month-to-month burn varied from $72,000 to $91,000, so he used his 3-month average of $80,000 for planning purposes.

Step 3: Apply the Basic Runway Formula

Runway (months) = Cash Balance ÷ Monthly Net Burn Rate

Marcus's calculation:
$640,000 ÷ $80,000 = 8 months of runway

This meant that with zero changes, Marcus would run out of cash in early September. Since it was January, his drop-dead date was crystal clear.

Step 4: Account for Revenue Growth (Advanced Runway Calculation)

The basic formula assumes flat revenue and burn, but most startups experience changes month over month. Marcus's revenue was growing 12% monthly, which would reduce his net burn rate over time.

For a more accurate projection with revenue growth, use this formula:

Months Until $0 = -LN(1 - (Cash Balance × Monthly Growth Rate) ÷ Current MRR) ÷ LN(1 + Monthly Growth Rate)

Common Runway Calculation Mistakes (That Can Kill Your Company)

Mistake 1: Assuming Flat Burn Rate

Marcus initially calculated runway assuming his $80,000 burn would stay constant. But he had planned hires that would increase burn to $105,000 by month 4. This would have reduced his runway from 8 months to 6.1 months—a critical difference.

Solution: Build a month-by-month cash flow projection that accounts for planned hiring, seasonal revenue changes, and known expense increases. Use our runway calculator that models dynamic burn rates automatically.

Mistake 2: Ignoring Accounts Payable in Runway Calculations

Marcus had $38,000 in outstanding payables that would hit his bank account within 30 days. Ignoring these artificially inflated his runway by 0.5 months.

Solution: Subtract near-term payables (due within 30 days) from your cash balance before calculating runway.

Mistake 3: Counting Verbal Commitments as Cash

The investor who backed out had verbally committed $500K. Marcus almost included this in his cash calculations, which would have shown 14+ months of runway (false).

Solution: Only count cash that has actually wired to your bank account. Cooley LLP's 2024 funding data shows that 23% of "soft circled" commitments fall through before closing.

Mistake 4: Forgetting About Debt Obligations

Marcus had taken a $200K venture debt loan with $8,333 monthly principal + interest payments. He initially calculated burn excluding this payment, understating his true cash consumption.

Solution: Include all cash outflows in burn rate, including debt service, even though loan principal technically isn't an "expense" from an accounting perspective.

Mistake 5: Overestimating Revenue Growth

Marcus's revenue was growing 12% monthly, and he projected this would continue indefinitely. In reality, growth rates typically compress as you scale. He should have used a conservative 8-10% growth assumption.

Solution: Use conservative revenue projections (lower end of your range) for runway planning. According to Redpoint Ventures' analysis, founder revenue projections are 2.3x too optimistic on average.

Frequently Asked Questions About Cash Runway

How much cash runway should a startup have?

Aim for 18-24 months of cash runway at all times. This provides sufficient buffer to fundraise from a position of strength (fundraising takes 4-6 months on average), weather unexpected challenges, and avoid desperate decision-making. According to Carta's data, startups with 18+ months runway are 4.3x more likely to successfully close their next funding round.

When should I start fundraising based on my runway?

Start fundraising when you have 12-18 months of runway remaining. This follows the "18-6-6 rule": begin with 18 months runway, expect 6 months for the fundraising process, and maintain 6 months buffer for delays. If your runway drops below 12 months, you're in yellow-flag territory and should accelerate your fundraising timeline immediately.

How do I extend my startup's runway without cutting headcount?

Focus on non-personnel cost reductions first: renegotiate SaaS subscriptions (average 22% savings), optimize cloud infrastructure (30-40% savings typical), reduce or eliminate paid marketing spend, downsize office space, and eliminate discretionary spending. Additionally, accelerate revenue through price increases, upsells to existing customers, and collecting outstanding receivables. These strategies can extend runway 30-50% without layoffs.

What's the difference between runway and burn rate?

Burn rate is how much cash you spend per month (expenses minus revenue), while runway is how many months until you run out of money (cash balance divided by burn rate). Burn rate is a velocity metric, runway is a time metric. For example, $80,000 monthly burn rate with $640,000 cash balance equals 8 months of runway.

Should I include accounts receivable in my runway calculation?

No, only include liquid cash that's currently in your bank accounts. Accounts receivable (money customers owe but haven't paid) should not be counted until it's actually received. Many startups fail despite having large receivables on paper because customers pay late or default entirely. Use cash-basis accounting for accurate runway calculations.

How do I calculate runway with growing revenue?

Use a dynamic runway model that accounts for month-over-month revenue growth reducing your net burn rate over time. The formula is complex (logarithmic calculation), so use a runway calculator that handles the math automatically. As a rule of thumb, if your revenue is growing 10%+ monthly, your actual runway is 15-25% longer than the simple calculation suggests.
